Background

About

I am a PhD candidate in Electrical & Computer Engineering at the National University of Singapore, where I work with Prof. BT Thomas Yeo and Prof. Thomas E. Nichols on AI for brain imaging. My research focuses on two closely connected questions: how to make complex biophysical brain models more efficient to study, and how to make biomedical AI model comparisons more statistically reliable.

Before starting my PhD, I studied in the Department of Automation at Tsinghua University — despite the name, its curriculum corresponds closely to what is called Electrical & Computer Engineering or EECS elsewhere, spanning signals and systems, control, machine learning, and computing. My undergraduate research included multi-agent trajectory prediction with neural networks, as well as projects in EEG analysis and DNA data encryption. Those experiences taught me to enjoy problems that sit between mathematical ideas, computation, and real systems. At NUS, that interest developed into work in computational neuroscience and AI for science.

I am currently learning more about large-scale model training, research infrastructure, foundation models and agent systems, and how to communicate technical work to broader audiences. The space that interests me most is where strong mathematical ideas become reliable, usable computational systems.

Selected Work

My research sits at the intersection of AI, brain imaging, computational neuroscience, and statistical methodology. I am interested in methods that are not only powerful, but also interpretable, computationally practical, and evaluated in a way that supports trustworthy scientific conclusions.

Nature Methods 2026 Accepted in principle

Optimizing biophysical large-scale brain circuit models with deep neural networks

Biophysical models let us reason mechanistically about the brain, from single-neuron dynamics up to large-scale circuits, and their parameters mean something biologically — many can even be measured directly. The unknown ones have to be fitted, and that is the bottleneck: every candidate parameter set requires numerically integrating the model's differential equations, which is far too slow for datasets the size of a population.

We built DELSSOME (Deep Learning for Surrogate Statistics Optimization in Mean field modeling), which skips the integration entirely and predicts directly whether a parameter set produces realistic brain dynamics. Across three large-scale circuit models it is 1500–8000× faster than numerical integration at that judgement, and dropped into an evolutionary optimization strategy it makes parameter estimation 50–100× faster without giving up agreement with numerical integration.

That changes what is affordable. Because of the compute cost, most studies simulate large-scale circuit models only at the group level; DELSSOME makes individual-level fitting of the feedback inhibition control (FIC) model practical. Collating 12,005 individuals across 14 datasets, we derive the first normative trajectories of cortical excitation–inhibition ratio across the lifespan, which reveal sex differences and network-specific patterns.

bioRxiv preprint 2026

Widespread use of invalid statistical tests in biomedical machine learning

Cross-validation is the default way to compare biomedical prediction algorithms — and to make downstream scientific claims, such as ranking biomarkers. But performance estimates across folds are not independent, and standard tests like the paired t-test assume that they are, which inflates false positive rates.

In a PRISMA-guided meta-analysis of 210 studies in journals with impact factor ≥ 15, we found that 97% ignored fold dependence. The problem spans scientific fields and is unaffected by impact factor, rigour-promoting policies, or open-science practices. We propose the SHARP (Split-HAlf RePeated) test, a small modification to cross-validation that makes the variance and the between-fold correlation separately estimable, and benchmark it against 12 existing tests.

In preparation Preprint forthcoming

Do brain foundation models perform better in phenotypic prediction than classical models?

Brain foundation models promise to transfer information across datasets and tasks. Whether that promise translates into better prediction of real phenotypes — rather than better numbers against a weak baseline — is an empirical question.

We benchmark brain foundation models against carefully tuned classical approaches such as kernel ridge regression on neuroscience prediction tasks, and ask under what conditions the additional complexity is justified.

Building in the open

Open Source

I actively build and release small, useful tools — mostly around AI agents, reproducibility, and doing real work in plain local files rather than behind another web account. The same instinct drives this as drives the research: a system is only worth trusting if you can see what it did and check it.

AI agents Node.js MIT

admissions-ops

A graduate-admissions workspace for AI agents. Give the agent an official program page and your own evidence files, and one prompt produces a sourced report, an eligibility matrix in which every unknown becomes a verification task instead of a guess, a tailored academic CV or admissions resume, and a statement draft — each claim traceable to an official URL or to something you actually wrote.

The architecture is the interesting part: the model authors every judgement, and a deterministic layer guards it. Thirteen validators reject anything that cannot be traced back to a source, so hallucinated claims and hand-edited artifacts fail the build, and nothing is marked final without human review. Everything stays as plain local files — no account, no server, no telemetry.

github.com/TianCZeng/admissions-ops

Contributions elsewhere — a merged pull request to career-ops, an open-source AI job-search agent workspace with 60k+ stars on GitHub.

More repositories are in progress — mostly teardowns and reimplementations of systems I want to understand properly, written up as I go. Issues and pull requests are welcome on anything here.
