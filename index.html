About

I am a PhD candidate in Electrical & Computer Engineering at the National University of Singapore, where I work with Prof. BT Thomas Yeo and Prof. Thomas E. Nichols on machine learning in brain imaging. My research focuses on two closely connected questions: how to make complex biophysical brain models more efficient to study, and how to make biomedical machine-learning comparisons more statistically reliable.

Before starting my PhD, I studied Automation at Tsinghua University. My undergraduate research included multi-agent trajectory prediction with neural networks, as well as projects in EEG analysis and DNA data encryption. Those experiences taught me to enjoy problems that sit between mathematical ideas, computation, and real systems. At NUS, that interest developed into work in computational neuroscience and scientific machine learning.

I care about research that is useful, reproducible, and honest about uncertainty. When a result is surprising, I like to examine the assumptions behind it, test strong baselines, and make the reasoning clear enough for someone outside the immediate project to follow. I also enjoy building practical tools and communicating technical ideas in a way that invites people into the problem.

How I work

I like starting with a precise question and a small experiment that can distinguish between competing explanations. I care about reproducibility because a result becomes more valuable when other people can understand, test, and build on it. In collaboration, I try to contribute careful implementation, clear communication, and connections between ideas that may initially seem to belong to different fields.

What I am learning

I am continuing to learn about large-scale model training, scientific machine learning, research infrastructure, and how to communicate technical work to broader audiences. I am especially interested in the space where strong mathematical ideas become reliable, usable computational systems.

Research

My research sits at the intersection of machine learning, brain imaging, computational neuroscience, and statistical methodology. I am interested in methods that are not only powerful, but also interpretable, computationally practical, and evaluated in a way that supports trustworthy scientific conclusions.

Research themes

Optimizing biophysical brain circuit models

Large-scale biophysical brain models can represent important aspects of brain organization, but they can also be expensive to simulate and optimize. I develop deep-learning methods that make these models more tractable, with the goal of enabling more efficient scientific investigation of complex brain systems.

Statistical validity in biomedical machine learning

Biomedical machine learning often compares many algorithms using cross-validation. I study whether the statistical procedures used in these comparisons support valid conclusions, and how seemingly reasonable evaluation choices can produce misleading rankings or overconfident claims.

Brain imaging and individual variation

I also work on methods for extracting meaningful structure from brain-imaging data, including brain parcellation and prediction of phenotypic traits. This work asks how computational models can capture individual and developmental variation without losing scientific interpretability.

Brain foundation models

Brain foundation models promise to transfer information across datasets and tasks. I am interested in evaluating whether these models actually improve phenotypic prediction over strong classical baselines, and in understanding when their additional complexity is justified.
